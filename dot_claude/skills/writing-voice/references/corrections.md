# Copyediting: Mechanical Errors to Catch

Fix these silently when drafting or revising — they are mechanics, not voice. These
are classes, not a checklist of strings; the examples illustrate each class, they do
not bound it.

**Boundary:** silent correction covers grammar, agreement, punctuation, spelling, and
consistency — nothing else. Never silently alter a number, formula, definition,
parameter value, citation, or claim. If something in those categories looks wrong,
leave the text intact and raise it separately as a question.

## Agreement and number

- Subject–verb agreement across intervening phrases: "the procedure applied to betas
  *is* applied," not "are applied." Watch compound predicates where the verb drifts to
  the nearest noun ("the mean is subtracted from the values *and the results are
  divided*," not "and are divided").
- Match plurals to their nouns: "the accounting *values* of debt, equity, and assets";
  "debt-to-equity and debt-to-assets *ratios*"; "J = 1 principal *component*."
- Latin plurals: criteria/criterion, phenomena/phenomenon, data/datum. Pick one plural
  of "index" (indexes or indices) per document, then keep it.

## Punctuation

- Punctuate introductory and participial phrases in both directions: add the missing
  comma ("Given common results from statistics, we can write...") and delete the stray
  one ("Once we've obtained...," not "Once, we've obtained..."). The missing comma is
  the more common failure.
- Never let a comma create a garden path: "the factor returns on day t *that* we are
  estimating," not "the factor returns on day t, we are estimating."
- Comma pairs open and close or don't exist ("and, for most exposures, are below 2").
  Close a parenthetical before the main clause resumes.
- No comma before "and" in a two-item compound; no spurious comma before a quoted term
  ("the term 'universal basic income'").

## Typos and proper nouns

- Proofread headings and captions separately — they escape both spell-check attention
  and the reader's ear, and a typo in display type is the most visible kind
  ("RECIPEINT" for "RECIPIENT").
- Give body text a read-aloud pass for dropped and mangled words: "principle" for
  "principal," "we are interest in," "a series cross-sectional regressions" (dropped
  "of").
- Capitalize parties, exchanges, and institutions correctly and identically each time:
  "Democratic nomination," "New York Stock Exchange."
- Verify company and vendor names against their own spelling: "Thomson Reuters," not
  "Thompson Reuters"; "McKinsey & Company."
- Delete acronym redundancy: "the IMI," not "the IMI index."
- Check verifiable facts in passing details — an offhand wrong detail (an exchange's
  opening time, a city's time zone) undermines trust in the numbers that matter.

## Consistency of terms, notation, and style

- One concept, one name, everywhere — heading, prose, variable, and figure. Not
  "Earnings Yield," "EARNINGS2PRICE," and "Earnings-to-Price" for the same factor.
- One capitalization for cross-references: "figure 4" or "Figure 4," never both; same
  for "equation" vs. "formula."
- One hyphenation: "trade-offs" or "tradeoffs"; "time series" or "time-series";
  "long-term" everywhere or nowhere.
- Never hyphenate an -ly adverb before a participle: "exponentially weighted,"
  "previously listed," "equally weighted."
- Number style consistent within a sentence and a document: "$1,000 per month"
  everywhere, not also "$1,000 monthly"; numerals or words for parallel values, not
  one of each.
- Units and windows consistent across parallel definitions: "the last 22 business
  days" and "the last 66 business days," not "the last month" and "the last 3 months."
- Equation lead-in punctuation: colon or period, chosen once, held throughout.
- Math notation self-consistent: subscripts present on every instance; matrix products
  in the same order across derivation steps; no index reused as its own summation
  limit; one capitalization per operator name and per variable.
- Prose names the object the equations define: if F is the covariance matrix and C the
  correlation matrix, prose cannot "augment the factor correlation matrix F."
- Matrix dimensions stated in prose must match the operations that follow: if
  x = Bᵀh and h is an N-vector, then B is N × K and Bᵀ is K × N.
- The prose definition contains everything the equation does: if the formula includes
  a shrinkage term, the defining sentence says so.

## Redundancy

- Redundant lead-ins: "takes the following block form," never "follows the following";
  "gives equation (34)," never "is the following result."
- A restatement that adds nothing gets cut: if a sentence opens "In summary," "In
  other words," or "This means that" and then says what the previous sentence already
  said — or says it at greater length — keep the better version and delete the other.
- One gloss per number: if a figure caption reports 7.3 versus 6.8, the body says what
  they mean rather than repeating both figures bare.
- One of each pair: "Additionally... also," "Upon visual inspection... we can see,"
  "solution solving," "would likely never."
- Deadweight openers cut on sight: "it is important to note that," "It is widely
  understood that," "It turns out that there is," "The fundamental idea is that,"
  "the issue of," "when it comes to," "in nature."
- Adverbs that assert instead of arguing: "intuitively set to zero," "cannot
  justifiably be interpreted," "properly evaluate" — delete the adverb.
- Word echo within a sentence or short paragraph ("literature... literature...
  literature"; "using... using") — reword one.

## Clarity

- Every pronoun gets an unmistakable antecedent that matches in number: "regardless of
  whether *those securities* were used," not "whether they were used."
- Fix dangling and stranded modifiers: "a shrinkage adjustment, proposed by Vasicek,
  that compensates for mean reversion," not "...mean reversion of betas as proposed in
  Vasicek." Attach each modifier to the verb or noun that takes it.
- Subjects get agency they can have: portfolios are not advice, models do not intend,
  phrasing does not ignore, figures do not explore, sections do not illustrate.
- Finish every comparative: "a superior psychological impact" needs *than what*; never
  compare portfolios to models.
- No double negatives: "no developed country has yet *conducted*," not "has yet to
  conduct" (which asserts the opposite).
- Lists stay parallel in part of speech: three noun phrases or three verb phrases, not
  two and one.
- One person per register: pronoun switches between registers are deliberate; drift
  within a passage ("we can see... one must consider... if you give people money") is
  not.
- One tense: a present-tense methods document does not say "the model focused."
- Replace the non-idiomatic: "as thus," "written down as," "equate them into," "ratio
  of X and Y" (→ of X to Y), "consider if" (→ whether), "the lack of data available"
  (→ of available data), "forgo any plans of" (→ plans to), "said data," "is had."
- Spell out comparison operators in prose: "at 30 or fewer observations," not "at
  <=30."
- Contorted passives get fixed ("is discussed later," never "discussion is had
  later") — but plain agentless passive is the correct register for specification
  prose. Never convert "Beta exposures are standardized globally" into a "we"
  construction, and never vary its wording across a catalog: the near-identical
  repetition is deliberate.

## Signposts must be true

- Every cross-reference points where it claims: "Recall from the previous section"
  must name the actual previous section; "equation (N)" is re-verified after any
  equation is inserted or removed.
- "In summary" and "Simply put" may only precede a summary. If new evidence, a new
  source, or a new figure follows, the signpost is a lie — delete it or move the
  paragraph.
- "As a result," "Therefore," and "This is because" must attach to something that
  actually produced the result or that the cited source actually asserts.
- One forward reference per passage: "discussed later" twice in two sentences means
  one sentence goes.
- Never duplicate a section title as its own first subsection heading.
- A roadmap and the headings that answer it use the same words ("argument" in the
  list and "arguments" in the heading is a mismatch readers notice).
- The piece's key metaphor is introduced in prose, not only in a figure caption.

## Citations and cross-artifact checks

- Spell every cited person, journal, and organization identically in the body and the
  source list, character for character. Get co-author order and affiliations right.
- Match dates between body and source list — a source dated 2017 is not "a 2015
  report."
- Match numbers between body, figure, and source: if the figure gives 400 million as
  the midpoint and 800 million as the maximum, the body cannot say "up to 400
  million."
- Every named author gets a citation a reader can follow, in one consistent form: one
  connector ("and," not sometimes "&"), one preposition ("proposed by," not sometimes
  "proposed in"), one placement (parenthetical or inline).
- Normalize the source list: one date format, months spelled out, one author-name
  form, one punctuation pattern, no "?." doubling, no missing period after "et al."
- A pull quote matches its source sentence character for character; if they differ,
  fix whichever is not the intended version. Never pull-quote the piece's own prose as
  though it were a source.
- Never let a footnote concede an error the text should fix: if the footnote says the
  equation is not really the CAPM, correct the equation; if it says the window is 252
  days against a body text saying 260, reconcile.
- Body framing must match the source's finding — don't lead with "45% favor" when the
  source is titled "More Americans oppose than favor."
